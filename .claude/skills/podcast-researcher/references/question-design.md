# Structure and question design

25 questions, sequenced so that a complete beginner and a working practitioner both
stay for two hours. Order matters more than cleverness: a brilliant question asked in
minute 4 gets a shallow answer, and the same question in minute 70 gets the story.

## The arc

Nine movements across ~120 minutes. Adjust counts to episode length; keep the order.

| # | Movement | Qs | Time | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Context setting | 2 | 8 min | who they are *now*, framed by the recent delta — not a résumé |
| 2 | Prior beliefs | 2 | 10 min | what they used to think; sets up the change |
| 3 | What changed in the market | 3 | 15 min | the "why now" that makes this episode timely |
| 4 | The tension | 2 | 12 min | evolution/contradiction, asked with curiosity |
| 5 | Concrete examples | 3 | 15 min | real cases, numbers, what actually happened |
| 6 | The framework, step by step | 5 | 25 min | the teaching core; must be implementable |
| 7 | Advanced implementation | 4 | 20 min | edge cases, scale, failure modes, what breaks |
| 8 | Predictions and risks | 2 | 10 min | forward-looking, but tied to their own evidence |
| 9 | Actionable next steps | 2 | 5 min | what the viewer does tomorrow morning |

Movement 6 is where a masterclass is either delivered or not. If the guest can't
give a step-by-step there, the angle was wrong.

Open on the recent delta rather than the origin story. The biography is available in
forty other interviews; starting there signals to a knowledgeable viewer that this
episode has nothing new.

## Question quality bar

A question earns its slot if it:

- **could only be asked of this guest** — it references something they specifically
  said, built, or changed
- **cannot be answered in one sentence**, but also isn't so broad it invites a monologue
- **is not on the kill list**
- **advances the arc** — it needs the previous answer to make sense
- **produces something the viewer can use**, or a moment they'll remember

Reject: "What is X?", "Where do you see this in 5 years?" as an opener, anything the
guest has a rehearsed 4-minute block for, and multi-part questions (the guest will
answer the easiest part and drop the rest).

Prefer the specific over the general: *"You said in <appearance, date> that ops is
where the advantage sits now — walk me through the last time that actually saved
you money"* beats *"How important are operations?"*

## What ships with every question

```markdown
### Q7 — <movement>
**Ask:** <the question, phrased the way the host actually talks>
**Why:** <what this unlocks; link to the brief evidence>
**Source:** <what they said/did that this is built on> [link, date]
**Likely answer:** <the canned response you expect>
**If canned →** <the follow-up that gets past it>
**If gold →** <where to dig deeper>
**Time:** ~<n> min
```

The "likely answer" field is what makes this useful live. Most guests have rehearsed
responses; predicting them in advance lets the host prepare the second question,
which is where the real material is.

Phrase the question in the host's own language and register — including Hinglish or
any mixed-language delivery. A question the host has to translate on the fly loses
its edge.

## Follow-up ladders

For the 8–10 questions that carry the episode, write a three-step ladder:

1. **Specificity** — "give me the actual number / the actual week that happened"
2. **Mechanism** — "what exactly had to be true for that to work?"
3. **Failure** — "when has that not worked, and what did you do instead?"

The failure rung is where most interviews stop early, and it's usually the most
valuable answer in the episode.

## Rescue kit

Include a short section in the runsheet for when the conversation stalls:

- **Guest gives short answers** → switch to "walk me through the day it happened"
- **Guest goes abstract** → "give me one company, one number, one date"
- **Guest repeats a rehearsed block** → "that's the version I've heard — what's the
  part you usually leave out because it takes too long?"
- **Segment dies early** → three backup questions per movement, held in reserve
- **Guest is bored** → jump to the excitement-spike topics from the guest brief

## Clip potential

Mark 5–6 questions as likely clip moments: a strong contrarian claim, a specific
number, a reversal, or a compressed framework. These are the ones worth restating
cleanly if the answer rambles ("so if I'm summarizing — ..."), because a clean
restatement is what makes a cuttable clip.

Don't optimize the whole episode for clips; optimize six moments and let the rest be
a real conversation.

## The runsheet

`05-runsheet.md` is one page the host holds while recording. Not the brief — the
brief is for the day before. The runsheet has:

- guest name, exact pronunciation, current title and company (spelled correctly)
- the episode promise in one line
- the 9 movements with time markers
- 25 questions as one line each, numbered
- kill list as a short "do not ask" strip
- landmines strip
- rescue kit
- the 5 clip moments starred

Big, scannable, no paragraphs. If it doesn't fit on one page it isn't a runsheet.
